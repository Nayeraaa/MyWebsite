Sustainable Financing of Communally Operated Social Service Provision (COSP)

Mutual AidHumanitarian SystemsConference Presentations ×2
Overview

During active conflict and severe state decay, traditional top-down humanitarian models often prove too rigid to provide the rapid, flexible response required for civilian survival. In Sudan, following the outbreak of war on April 15, 2023, local communities rapidly filled this institutional vacuum by establishing Emergency Response Rooms (ERRs) — atomized, neighborhood-level units of mutual aid. Grounded in deep historical African traditions of collective responsibility and solidarity, such as ubuntu and harambee, these horizontal networks reject traditional charity dynamics in favor of reciprocity, where all community members can both contribute and benefit. ERRs have taken on the survival-critical work of managing communal kitchens, healthcare clinics, evacuation coordination, and basic utility maintenance.

While these initiatives successfully sustained millions in their first year, they face extreme funding instability. Initially operating on host-community mutual support and diaspora donations, ERRs are highly vulnerable as local and global financial resources are depleted by a prolonged war. Attempts to secure institutional funding from international non-governmental organizations (INGOs) are severely bottlenecked by rigid, Eurocentric bureaucratic requirements. To bypass these barriers, ERRs organized into the Localization Coordination Council (LCC) and engineered the "F System" — a decentralized, five-phase contractual and reporting process (F1–F5) that streamlines planning, peer-based vetting, and financial reporting while integrating participatory community accountability directly into local governance.

To move beyond a precarious "firefighting" mode, this study explores alternative, sustainable financing models that leverage local community assets. Rather than treating aid as a one-way donation, proposed models suggest leveraging diaspora and community contributions as investments in local, value-added economic activities. By funding community-run productive assets — such as agricultural processing (producing oils and butters), micro-scale food processing, community shops, transportation services, or implementing nominal service registration fees — ERRs can generate cyclical, self-sustaining revenue pools. Integrating communal governance with productive capacity-building offers a viable pathway toward the democratization and long-term resilience of social service provision during state failure.
