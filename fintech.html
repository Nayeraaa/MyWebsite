Digital Payment Technology in Sudan

FinTechDigital Public Infrastructure
Overview

Sudan's financial technology ecosystem is undergoing a rapid, crisis-driven transformation under the dual pressures of active conflict and monetary instability. Following the outbreak of war in April 2023, the destruction of major physical data centers and the collapse of traditional banking operations forced millions of citizens to rely on digital alternatives. The Bank of Khartoum's Bankak app became the dominant transaction and remittance channel, demonstrating remarkable digital resilience as a lifeline for moving money. This shift was further accelerated by the 2024/25 currency note change, which restricted physical cash circulation to an all-time low and turned digital payment solutions from a convenience into an absolute necessity.

Analyzing this landscape through the Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI) framework reveals deep structural inequities and system-level vulnerabilities. Historically, Sudan's financial system was highly exclusive; because only 15% of adults were banked pre-war, banking apps tied to formal accounts structurally excluded 85% of the population. While mobile money could bridge this gap, its expansion has been severely hindered because telecom operators and banks failed to incentivize local cash-in/cash-out agents. These crucial agents often redirect their liquidity elsewhere because they earn much higher, unregulated commissions (10% to 50%) from informal airtime transfers than from formal mobile money fees. Furthermore, the Electronic Banking Services (EBS), which operates the national switch, acts as a critical single point of failure. EBS lost access to its core systems during the early stages of the war, exposing a severe lack of disaster recovery measures.

To build an inclusive and resilient digital payment ecosystem, Sudan must transition from fragmented workarounds to systemic regulatory reforms. Although the Central Bank of Sudan (CBoS) took positive steps by enabling Basic Bank Account Number (BBAN) interoperability to ease the cash crisis, the broader regulatory and legal framework remains outdated and fragmented. The policy paper recommends that Sudan establish robust disaster recovery plans, resolve ongoing bank-telecom interoperability issues, and implement comprehensive data protection and digital identity laws. It also emphasizes the importance of building public trust, warning that state-led technological interventions fail without citizen buy-in. Finally, the paper highlights Sudan's regional isolation, noting that it is one of only five nations that have not ratified the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) agreement, thereby missing out on key regional digital trade and payment protocols.
